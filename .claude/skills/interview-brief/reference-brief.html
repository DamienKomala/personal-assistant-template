What changed since you applied

The referral came from the operator, not a contact

This section exists because researching people is the highest-value step in the skill. In the real run this template came from, a contact recorded in the ledger as nothing but an email address turned out to be the company’s Chief Operating Officer — which reframed the entire opportunity.

Write what the lookup actually changed: the person’s title, tenure and route into the role, and what that means for how the file was opened. Then say plainly what it does and does not buy — a strong referral means the recruiter opened the file knowing who vouched; it does not mean the job is won.

Still owed, and it is yours

Anything owed to a friend, a client or a personal referral contact goes here, named and undrafted. The skill never writes those messages.

Where this call sits

One of three rounds, and the shortest

  1. 01 Recruiter screen — background, motivation, logistics, comp range You are here
  2. 02 Hiring manager — the person who owns this reporting line
  3. 03 Panel — the wider team

Numbering is used here because the rounds are a real sequence. Do not number sections that are not.

Calibrate for the room you are actually in. A career recruiter runs a fit-and-filter conversation, not a craft interview — lead with clarity and sector fluency, and save the deep reasoning for round two. State which kind of interviewer this is and what follows from it.

The company, in terms you can use

What they sell, and what shipped recently

Two or three sentences on the business — what it does, for whom, roughly how big, how funded. Then the part that earns its place:

Anything shipped in the last six months, especially if the role is named for it. A brief written from company boilerplate misses this, and it is usually the most useful thing in the document.

  • Product line one — what it does, in the customer’s vocabulary rather than the company’s.
  • Product line two — with a real unit or figure where one exists: a domain term, a throughput number, a document type.
  • Product line three — and the shape underneath, if several share one.

Close by naming the underlying problem shape and tying it to real prior work, so the fit section has somewhere to land.

The fit

What they asked for, and what you say

Quote the requirement verbatim from the posting, then answer it from the profile and impact files. Never a generic strength, never an invented one.

“A requirement, quoted exactly as the posting words it”
The specific prior work that answers it, named — employer, product, outcome. Lead with the outcome rather than the artefact.
“A second requirement, quoted”
A different piece of real experience. One claim, one story — repeating the same project across four rows reads as a thin record.
“A requirement with no genuine match”
Say so. Give the honest bridge — adjacent experience, or a plain statement that it is a gap. Manufacturing a match here is the one failure this skill cannot recover from.

Address the level question before they do

Where experience substantially exceeds the stated bar, that invites a quiet question about level and cost. Answer it with scope rather than seniority: name what the posting says the role owns, and say that is what you want to own.

Money

Read the band, do not anchor on it

State what the band actually includes — base only, base plus bonus, or base plus bonus plus equity. A very wide spread on one requisition usually means the ceiling is level- and bonus-loaded rather than reachable.

Then give the one useful comparison: how the floor sits against the goal figure. That is enough for a first call.

  • If asked for expectations on a first call, the published band is the answer. Nothing is gained by naming a number inside it on day one.
  • Keep prior compensation in reserve. Several US states bar an employer from asking salary history while allowing the applicant to volunteer it — where that holds, it is worth more late than early. Jurisdiction-specific, and not legal advice.
  • Where the law provides it, a pay scale can be requested in writing after an initial interview. Useful when several conversations pass with no figure.

Your questions

Ask these

Calibrate to the interviewer’s actual role. Asking a recruiter about team structure is fine; asking them to defend a technical trade-off is not.

Which surface does this role pick up first?
Say what the answer would tell them. A question without a stated purpose is filler.
Who does this role report to, and how big is the team today?
Confirms who round two is with — a recruiter can always answer this.
What does the panel round look like — a presentation, or a working session?
Practical, and it means preparing the right thing rather than both.

Watch for

Four things not to do

  • Competitive positioning stays in round two. Knowing the landscape is an asset when asked and a liability when volunteered — in a screen it reads as positioning against a would-be employer.
  • Do not oversell tooling. Naming a limitation you have designed around is stronger than listing tools you have used.
  • Do not lean on a referral twice. Mention it once, warmly, early — then let the conversation stand on its own.
  • Keep the recording in mind if there is one. The transcript is the artefact that reaches the panel.

Four is about right. More reads as nagging, and the section loses its force.
